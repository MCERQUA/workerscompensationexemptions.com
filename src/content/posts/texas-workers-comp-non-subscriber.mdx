Texas stands alone in the United States: it is the only state that does not require private employers to carry workers' compensation insurance. This makes Texas a unique and often misunderstood environment for business owners trying to manage their workers' comp costs and obligations.

This guide explains the Texas non-subscriber system in plain English — what it means to opt out, what tort defenses non-subscribers lose, what occupational accident insurance is and whether it helps, what the posting requirements are, and what injured workers' rights look like when their employer has no coverage.

## What Is a Texas Non-Subscriber?

A Texas employer that chooses not to purchase workers' compensation insurance is called a "non-subscriber." This is not an exemption in the traditional sense — it's a complete opt-out from the Texas workers' comp system.

Non-subscribers are not required to carry workers' comp, notify the state in advance that they're opting out, or follow the Texas Department of Insurance Workers' Compensation Division (TDI-DWC) system's claim-handling procedures.

However — and this is critical — non-subscribers lose nearly all the tort defenses that make workers' comp such a valuable protection for employers.

## What Tort Defenses Does a Non-Subscriber Lose?

Workers' compensation is often called the "grand bargain" of American labor law: employees give up the right to sue their employer for work injuries in exchange for guaranteed medical and wage benefits; employers give up the right to defend themselves on the merits of negligence in exchange for limited, predictable liability.

When a Texas employer opts out of workers' comp, they give up the benefits of the grand bargain — specifically, they can no longer claim:

**1. Contributory negligence of the employee.** Under common law, if an injured worker contributed to their own injury (for example, by not following safety procedures), that contributory negligence can reduce or eliminate their recovery. Texas non-subscribers cannot use this defense.

**2. Assumption of the risk.** Non-subscribers cannot argue that the worker knew about and accepted the risks of the job.

**3. Fellow employee negligence.** Non-subscribers cannot argue that the injury was caused by a coworker's negligence rather than the employer's.

Without these defenses, an injured worker can sue a Texas non-subscriber employer for the full value of their injuries — past and future medical costs, past and future lost wages, pain and suffering, and potentially punitive damages in egregious cases. A workplace injury that would have cost a covered employer $30,000-$50,000 in workers' comp benefits could cost a non-subscriber $300,000 or more in a jury verdict.

## Who Chooses Non-Subscriber Status?

Despite the liability exposure, some Texas businesses deliberately choose non-subscriber status. Common reasons include:

**Lower immediate cost:** Workers' comp premiums can be substantial, especially for businesses in high-hazard industries. Non-subscription avoids that premium cost.

**Control over claims management:** Non-subscribers handle their own injury claims, which can mean more control over medical vendor selection, return-to-work programs, and settlement decisions.

**Occupational accident insurance as an alternative:** Many Texas non-subscribers purchase occupational accident insurance as a lower-cost alternative that provides some — but not all — of the protection workers' comp provides.

**Low injury risk:** Some businesses with low injury frequency and severity calculate that the probability-weighted cost of an injury lawsuit is lower than the cost of annual workers' comp premiums.

This calculus can work for some businesses, but it only needs to fail once — one serious injury can produce a judgment that exceeds years of premium savings.

## What Is Occupational Accident Insurance?

Occupational accident insurance (often called "occ-acc") is a private insurance product that provides medical and disability benefits for work-related injuries. It is popular among Texas non-subscribers as a way to provide some protection for injured workers without carrying formal workers' comp.

**How occ-acc differs from workers' comp:**

- Occ-acc is not workers' comp and does not trigger the grand bargain. An injured worker covered by occ-acc can still sue the employer in addition to receiving occ-acc benefits.
- Occ-acc policies have defined benefit limits — a specific maximum for medical expenses, a specific weekly disability benefit — rather than the open-ended statutory benefits workers' comp provides.
- Occ-acc claims are adjudicated privately, not through the TDI-DWC system.
- Occ-acc coverage does not protect against all forms of employer liability the way workers' comp does.

**When occ-acc is appropriate:**

Occupational accident insurance is most appropriate for:
- Texas non-subscribers who want to provide some benefits to injured workers while managing premium costs
- Businesses that use legitimate independent contractors who want protection for work-related injuries
- Employers supplementing workers' comp with additional coverage for gaps

Occ-acc is NOT a legal substitute for workers' comp for employees in any state other than Texas. Using occ-acc instead of workers' comp for employees outside Texas is itself a compliance violation.

## DBA vs. Workers' Comp for Government Contractors

A related concept is the Defense Base Act (DBA), which applies to private contractors working on U.S. government contracts outside the United States. DBA is a federal law requiring employers to provide workers' comp-equivalent coverage for workers on covered government contracts.

DBA coverage is administered through commercial insurance companies but follows the federal workers' comp system under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act. If your Texas business performs work on U.S. government contracts overseas, DBA coverage is mandatory — non-subscriber status does not exempt you from federal DBA requirements.

## Texas Non-Subscriber Posting Requirements

Texas non-subscribers have specific legal obligations regarding notice to employees:

**Workplace posting:** Non-subscribers must post a notice in the workplace informing employees that the employer is not covered by workers' comp. This notice must be posted in a conspicuous location — a break room, near the time clock, or another place where employees will regularly see it.

**Language requirements:** The posting must be in English and Spanish (and any other language spoken by a significant portion of the workforce).

**Content requirements:** The notice must inform employees of their right to sue the employer for work-related injuries under common law.

Failure to post the required notice doesn't change the non-subscriber's liability — but it can make the business look worse to a jury, which is the opposite of what you want in a lawsuit.

**At hiring:** Texas non-subscribers should also inform new employees at the time of hire that the business does not carry workers' comp. This is good practice and helps avoid surprise and resentment when a worker is injured and discovers there's no workers' comp claim to file.

## Injured Worker Rights Under Non-Subscriber

An employee of a Texas non-subscriber who is injured at work has the right to:

1. **Sue the employer in civil court** for damages, without the contributory negligence, assumption of risk, or fellow employee defenses available to covered employers
2. **Pursue any occ-acc benefits the employer provides**, if the employer carries occupational accident insurance
3. **Seek treatment through their personal health insurance**, though health insurers may have work-injury exclusions or subrogation rights against the employer

The injured worker cannot file a TDI-DWC workers' comp claim — that system only applies to employers with coverage.

## The Bottom Line for Texas Businesses

Texas non-subscriber status is a legitimate choice — but it's a choice with real financial consequences if an injury occurs. Before opting out of workers' comp:

1. Honestly assess your workplace injury risk
2. Calculate the cost of occ-acc coverage as an alternative
3. Understand the tort defenses you lose
4. Post the required notices
5. Train your supervisors on injury response procedures (since you'll be managing claims yourself)
6. Consider how you would pay a six-figure jury verdict

For businesses with consistent, predictable injury patterns and the cash reserves to handle an adverse outcome, non-subscriber status can work. For most businesses, particularly those with moderate-to-high injury exposure, the cost of workers' comp is the better hedge against a catastrophic loss.
