If you've ever tried to figure out whether your LLC members need to be covered by workers' comp, you've likely run into the same wall: the rules are completely different depending on which state you're in. One state exempts LLC members automatically; another requires them to be covered; a third doesn't even mandate workers' comp at all. This guide cuts through the confusion.

## Why LLC Member Exemptions Are So Complicated

Workers' compensation is regulated at the state level — not the federal level — which means each of the 50 states has its own rules about who must be covered, who can be excluded, and how to file an exemption. For LLC members specifically, the rules vary on:

- Whether LLC members can exempt themselves at all
- Whether there's a cap on the number of members who can be exempt
- Whether exemptions apply differently to single-member vs. multi-member LLCs
- Whether industry (especially construction) changes the rules
- Whether the LLC is manager-managed or member-managed

Understanding these dimensions is essential before you assume your LLC members are — or aren't — covered.

## Florida: Annual Filing, Construction Restrictions, 10-Member Cap

Florida is one of the states with the highest volume of LLC member exemption filings nationally, particularly in the construction industry. Here's how it works:

**Non-construction LLCs:** LLC members who are officers of non-construction businesses can apply for an exemption through the Florida Division of Workers' Compensation. The exemption must be renewed annually — missing the renewal means the exemption lapses and the member immediately becomes subject to coverage requirements.

**Construction LLCs:** The rules are stricter. Florida limits exemptions in the construction industry to corporate officers, not LLC members in the traditional sense. The state caps construction industry exemptions at 10 per company. This is a significant distinction: a construction LLC with 12 active members cannot have all 12 exempt.

**Annual renewal:** Unlike some states where an exemption remains in effect until revoked, Florida requires annual renewal. If your renewal date passes without action, your exemption is gone — and you may be out of compliance immediately. Calendar your renewal dates.

## California: AB5 Complicates Everything

California is one of the strictest states for workers' comp, and LLC member exemptions are severely limited.

**The basic rule:** California generally does not allow LLC members to exempt themselves from workers' comp simply because they are LLC members. If you want an exemption as an LLC member in California, you typically must qualify as a corporate officer of a corporation — which means converting your LLC to a corporation first.

**AB5's impact:** California's AB5 law, which codified the ABC test for independent contractor classification, has made it harder to use 1099 contractor relationships as a way to avoid workers' comp obligations. Many businesses that previously paid workers as independent contractors have had to reclassify them as employees and provide coverage. If your LLC uses subcontractors in California, you need to understand the ABC test: the worker must (A) be free from your control, (B) perform work outside your usual course of business, and (C) be engaged in an independently established business.

**Enforcement:** California's Department of Industrial Relations (DIR) is aggressive about workers' comp enforcement. Penalties for non-compliance include stop-work orders, criminal referrals, and fines of $10,000 or more per employee per violation. Don't assume an LLC structure alone provides any protection in California.

## Texas: No Mandatory Coverage — But Watch the Non-Subscriber Exposure

Texas is the outlier: it is the only state in the country that does not require private employers to carry workers' comp insurance. LLC members in Texas don't need to file an exemption because there is no mandatory coverage requirement to exempt themselves from.

However, "no mandatory coverage" is not the same as "no risk." Texas employers who choose not to carry workers' comp — called "non-subscribers" — lose key tort defenses:

- They cannot claim the injured worker assumed the risk
- They cannot claim the worker's own negligence caused the injury
- They cannot use fellow-employee immunity as a defense

This means an injured worker can sue a Texas non-subscriber for the full value of their injuries under common law. A workplace injury that would cost a covered employer $50,000 in workers' comp benefits could cost a non-subscriber hundreds of thousands of dollars in a lawsuit. Most Texas businesses with employees carry either traditional workers' comp or occupational accident insurance as an alternative — even though neither is legally required.

## Arizona: 10% Stock Ownership Rule

Arizona requires workers' comp for all employers with one or more employees. Corporate officers — and LLC members who are treated as officers — can be excluded from coverage if they own at least 10% of the company.

**The 10% rule applies to LLC members:** In Arizona, an LLC member can elect to be excluded from workers' comp if they own at least a 10% membership interest. This is a relatively accessible threshold for small LLCs where members are active in the business.

**Industrial Commission of Arizona (ICA) oversight:** The ICA enforces Arizona's workers' comp requirements. Construction businesses in Arizona face heightened scrutiny on subcontractor classification and exemption eligibility.

**Multi-state operations:** An Arizona exemption covers work performed in Arizona. If your LLC members work in other states, you need to understand each state's rules separately.

## Single-Member vs. Multi-Member LLCs

One dimension that's easy to overlook is the difference between single-member and multi-member LLCs:

**Single-member LLCs** are often treated like sole proprietors in many states — automatically excluded from mandatory coverage requirements. In these states, you don't need to file an exemption; you're simply not required to cover yourself. But if you want coverage for yourself, you can typically elect to purchase it.

**Multi-member LLCs** are more complex. Some states exempt all active members; others limit exemptions to a set number; others require each member to file individually. The manager-managed vs. member-managed distinction also matters in some states — states may limit exemptions to members who actively manage the business.

## The Renewal Problem: Why Good Exemptions Go Bad

One of the most common — and costly — LLC member exemption mistakes is missing the renewal deadline. Many states require annual or biennial renewal, and when a renewal lapses:

- The exemption voids automatically
- The member is immediately treated as an employee subject to coverage requirements
- If no policy is in place, the business may be out of compliance with the state's mandatory coverage laws
- If a workplace injury occurs during the lapsed period, the business faces personal liability for the injury

The solution is a tracking system — calendar reminders for every exemption renewal date, assigned to someone who won't let it fall through the cracks.

## What to Do Before Filing

Before you file an LLC member exemption in any state, work through these questions:

1. What state are you filing in, and what does that state specifically require for LLC member exemptions?
2. Is your business in an industry with stricter rules (construction, especially)?
3. Is your LLC single-member or multi-member, and does the state treat those differently?
4. Is your LLC manager-managed or member-managed?
5. When is the renewal deadline, and who is responsible for tracking it?
6. What happens to a member who is injured while they are exempt — do they have any coverage?

If you're operating in multiple states, you need to repeat this analysis for each state where members perform work.

## The Bottom Line

LLC member workers' comp exemptions are not a national standard — they're 50 different state systems. Getting the exemption right requires knowing your specific state's rules, filing correctly, and renewing on time. Getting it wrong can mean retroactive premium assessments, penalties, and personal liability for a worker's injuries.

If you're not certain about your state's current rules, a quick conversation with an advisor who specializes in workers' comp exemptions is the fastest way to get clarity — before an auditor shows up with questions.
